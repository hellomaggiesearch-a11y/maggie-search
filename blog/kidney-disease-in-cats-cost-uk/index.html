Kidney Disease in Cats: What the Medication Really Costs in the UK (And How to Pay Less)

If your vet has just used the words "kidney" and "chronic" in the same sentence, the floor probably dropped a little. It is one of the most common diagnoses in older cats — and one of the most manageable, once you understand what you are dealing with and what it will cost.

Maggie Search Editorial Team · Published 30 August 2026 · Last updated 30 August 2026

Here is the part owners are rarely told plainly at that first appointment: chronic kidney disease in cats is managed, not cured. The kidney function that has been lost does not come back. But with the right treatment, a good diet and regular monitoring, a cat can live well for years after the diagnosis.

That also means a cost that repeats every month, often for the rest of the cat's life — and nobody hands you that number as you leave the consulting room.

This guide walks through what chronic kidney disease actually involves, the signs worth acting on, the medicines your cat is likely to need, and — the part most owners never get told — why the same prescription can cost very different amounts from one UK pharmacy to the next, and how to pay less without ever compromising your cat's care.

What Chronic Kidney Disease in Cats Actually Is

The kidneys are the body's filtration system. They clear waste products from the blood, balance hydration and minerals, and help regulate blood pressure. It is quiet, constant work, and cats have a great deal of spare kidney capacity in reserve.

In chronic kidney disease (CKD) that capacity is gradually and permanently lost. Unlike acute kidney injury, which comes on suddenly, CKD is slow and progressive — which is exactly why it hides for so long. By the time the outward signs appear, a cat has often already lost a large share of working kidney tissue.

It is strikingly common. Studies suggest CKD affects roughly one in three cats over the age of ten, and it is one of the leading medical causes of death in older cats. The good news buried in that statistic: caught early and managed well, it is one of the conditions where consistent treatment makes the biggest difference.

Which cats are most at risk

  • Age: risk climbs steadily from around seven years and is highest in senior and geriatric cats
  • Breed: some pedigrees are more prone — Persians and related breeds (polycystic kidney disease), Maine Coons, Abyssinians and others
  • Chronic dehydration: cats have a naturally low thirst drive, and years of running slightly dry places extra strain on the kidneys

If your cat is over seven, an annual blood and urine test should be part of a routine check. It is not fussing — early detection is the single thing most likely to buy extra good years.

The Signs Worth Acting On

The frustrating thing about CKD is how subtle it is at the start. The earliest signs are easy to read as "just getting older":

  • Drinking more than usual, and emptier water bowls
  • Larger, more frequent wet patches in the litter tray
  • Gradual weight loss, often while still eating normally at first
  • Reduced appetite or fussiness with previously loved food
  • Occasional vomiting that can seem random
  • Bad breath, sometimes with an unusual ammonia-like smell
  • Lethargy — sleeping more, engaging less
  • A dull, unkempt coat and visible dehydration

If you have noticed any of these — particularly increased drinking and urinating in an older cat — ask your vet for blood and urine tests. Early detection genuinely changes the outcome.

The IRIS Stages: Knowing Where Your Cat Stands

Vets stage CKD using the international IRIS system, based mainly on blood creatinine (and often SDMA), with urine protein and blood pressure added on top. Knowing the stage explains why your cat is on the treatment they are on.

IRIS stageWhat it meansSymptoms?Typical approach
1Earliest change, kidney values still near normalNone or very mildMonitor, address hydration and diet
2Mild loss of functionOften subtle or unnoticedRenal diet begins; treat proteinuria/blood pressure
3Moderate loss of functionVisible — thirst, weight loss, nauseaActive medication and diet, closer monitoring
4Advanced loss of functionMarked — poor appetite, weaknessFocus on comfort and quality of life

You can only know the stage from a blood and urine test. If your cat is over seven, asking for that test yearly is preventive, inexpensive relative to what it prevents, and occasionally life-changing.

The Medications — and Why the Active Ingredient Matters More Than the Brand

This is the part that confuses most newly diagnosed owners, and it is also where the money is. Knowing the active ingredient, not just the brand name, is the single most useful thing you can learn. The active ingredient is what treats your cat; the same active ingredient often appears under more than one brand, at very different prices.

CKD is rarely one tablet. It is usually a small, tailored combination, adjusted as the disease progresses.

Protecting the kidneys — telmisartan and benazepril

Where a cat is losing protein into the urine (proteinuria) or has raised blood pressure, vets commonly reach for telmisartan (Semintra), an angiotensin-receptor blocker licensed in the UK for exactly this, or an ACE inhibitor such as benazepril (Fortekor). Both reduce protein loss and ease the pressure inside the kidney, slowing progression. Given daily, long term, with periodic blood tests.

Fortekor is an originator brand; benazepril is also available as VMD-authorised alternatives. Same active ingredient, different label, often a different price.

Appetite and nausea — mirtazapine and maropitant

A cat that will not eat loses ground quickly, so much of CKD care is about keeping appetite up and nausea down:

  • Mirtazapine (including Mirataz, a transdermal ear gel): stimulates appetite and reduces nausea
  • Maropitant (Cerenia): controls vomiting and nausea

These are quality-of-life medicines — cats on them tend to eat better and vomit less, which matters enormously day to day.

Diet, phosphate and supplements

Alongside prescription medicines, most CKD cats are managed with:

  • A veterinary renal diet (controlled phosphate and protein) — one of the best-evidenced parts of treatment
  • Phosphate binders such as Ipakitine or Pronefra, when diet alone is not enough
  • Support for hydration and, in some cats, potassium or B-vitamins, which can be lost through the kidneys

Anti-inflammatories such as meloxicam (Metacam) are sometimes used for coexisting arthritis, but only cautiously and under close veterinary supervision in a cat with CKD.

The Number Nobody Shows You

A cat with moderate-to-advanced CKD is commonly on two or three medicines plus a prescription diet, every day, indefinitely — with periodic blood tests, urine checks and blood-pressure readings on top. It is a real, repeating monthly commitment.

And here is where many owners make the single most dangerous decision available to them: they stretch doses to make a pack last longer, skip the odd day, or simply buy whatever came up first without checking. Missing doses does not save money — it undoes the very thing you are paying for, and a crisis costs far more than any pack.

The answer is not less medicine. It is paying less for the same medicine. There are clear structural reasons why the same pet medicine costs different amounts across UK pharmacies, and understanding them turns a monthly shock into a managed budget.

Ways to Pay Less — Without Compromising Care

1. Ask your vet for a written prescription

In the UK you are legally entitled to ask your vet for a written prescription and buy the medication from a VMD-registered pharmacy of your choice. A vet cannot refuse to write one for a medicine they are already prescribing to your animal.

Buying from your own practice's dispensary is convenient. It is frequently not the cheapest option, and for a lifelong daily treatment this one request is the largest single saving available to most owners.

2. Ask about the same active ingredient under a different brand

Because several of these medicines are available from more than one manufacturer, there may be an equally suitable, VMD-authorised product with the same active ingredient at a lower price. Only your vet can decide what is appropriate, and a UK prescription is written for a specific product — but you are entitled to the conversation. "Is there an equivalent with the same active ingredient that might cost less?" is a fair, sensible question.

3. Sort out insurance before a diagnosis, not after

Once CKD is on the record it becomes a pre-existing condition, and cover for it becomes very hard to arrange. If you have a younger cat, or an at-risk breed, this matters now rather than later. It is worth weighing the honest pros and cons of pet insurance for older pets while you still have the choice.

Simple Things That Help at Home

Beyond medication, a few everyday habits genuinely slow the decline and keep your cat comfortable.

Get more water into your cat

  • Offer several water bowls around the house, away from food and the litter tray
  • Try a pet water fountain — many cats drink far more from moving water
  • Lean towards wet food, which carries a lot of moisture with every meal

Feed the right diet

  • Ask your vet about a veterinary renal diet with controlled phosphate and protein
  • Introduce any new food gradually — CKD cats are famously fussy
  • Warming wet food slightly can make it more tempting for a flagging appetite

Monitor and keep things calm

  • Keep to the blood and urine re-checks your vet recommends
  • Weigh your cat regularly — steady weight loss is an early warning of progression
  • Minimise stress and keep routines predictable; comfort is part of the treatment

Frequently Asked Questions

How long can a cat live with chronic kidney disease?

It depends heavily on the IRIS stage at diagnosis and how consistently the cat is treated. Many cats diagnosed early live comfortably for several years with medication, a renal diet and regular monitoring. Cats found at stage 2, before obvious symptoms, generally do far better than those diagnosed at stage 3 or 4.

What are the first signs of kidney disease in cats?

The earliest signs are usually drinking more and urinating more, often with gradual weight loss despite a normal appetite. They are easy to miss, which is why a blood and urine test from around seven years of age is the single most useful thing you can do.

Can chronic kidney disease in cats be cured?

No. CKD is managed, not cured — the loss of kidney function is permanent. But treatment slows its progression, controls the symptoms and can give a cat years of good quality life, which is why it continues for life.

Can I buy Semintra or Fortekor without a prescription in the UK?

No. Telmisartan (Semintra) and benazepril (Fortekor) are POM-V medicines and legally require a veterinary prescription. You can, however, ask your vet for a written prescription and buy from any VMD-registered pharmacy rather than the practice dispensary.

How much does kidney medication for a cat cost in the UK?

It depends on the medicines prescribed, your cat's stage and where you buy. What is consistent is the spread: the same product can differ substantially in price between pharmacies, and originator brands usually cost more than VMD-authorised alternatives with the same active ingredient.

Should I stop my cat's medication if she seems better?

No. Feeling better is the treatment working. Stopping or spacing doses lets the underlying decline and the symptoms return. Only your vet should change the dose or the plan.

What To Do This Week

  • Book a blood and urine test if your senior cat is drinking or urinating more.
  • Ask your vet for a written prescription so you are free to compare prices.
  • Write down the active ingredient, not just the brand — it is your key to finding equivalents.
  • Add a second water bowl or a fountain today, and move towards wet food.
  • Compare the price of your cat's exact medication across UK pharmacies before your next refill.
  • Review insurance — especially for any other pets not yet diagnosed.

⚠️ Never change kidney treatment without your vet

Never start, stop, space out or substitute your cat's medication, renal diet or fluid therapy without your vet's explicit guidance. CKD treatment is finely balanced to your individual cat, and sudden changes can do harm. This article helps you pay less for the treatment your vet has prescribed — not replace their advice.

The Bottom Line

A kidney disease diagnosis is frightening, but this is one of the conditions where steady, consistent management pays off most. Caught at the right stage, treated properly and monitored regularly, many cats go on for years — eating, purring and claiming the warm spot on the sofa exactly as they always did.

What makes the difference is not luck. It is consistency: the daily medicine, the renal diet, the water within reach, the re-check booked. And yes — the treatment fitting your budget every month, because the treatment you can sustain is the only one that works.
