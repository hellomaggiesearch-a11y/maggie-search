Heart Failure in Dogs: What the Medication Really Costs in the UK (And 3 Ways to Pay Less)

If your vet has just listened to your dog's chest and gone quiet for a second longer than usual, you already know the feeling. A murmur. Then a scan. Then a word nobody wants attached to their dog: heart failure.

Maggie Search Editorial Team · Published 7 August 2026 · Last updated 7 August 2026

Here is the part most owners are not told plainly in that first consultation: heart disease in dogs is managed, not cured. Once your dog starts cardiac medication, they stay on it. Every day, at the same times, for the rest of their life. Stopping is dangerous.

That means a cost that repeats every single month for years — and nobody hands you that number when you leave the practice.

This guide does. Below you will find what heart failure in dogs actually involves, why the same prescription can cost very different amounts from one pharmacy to the next, and three legitimate ways to reduce what you pay without ever compromising your dog's care.

What Heart Failure in Dogs Actually Is

Your dog's heart has valves that work like one-way doors: blood goes through, the door shuts, blood moves forward.

In myxomatous mitral valve disease (MMVD) — by far the most common cause in dogs — the valve on the left side gradually thickens and deforms. It stops closing properly. With every beat, a little blood leaks backwards instead of moving on.

That leak makes a sound your vet hears through the stethoscope: a heart murmur. It is almost always the first sign, and it appears long before your dog looks unwell.

Over time the heart enlarges to cope with the extra volume. When it can no longer compensate, fluid begins to build up in the lungs. That is when the cough, the tiredness and the breathlessness appear — congestive heart failure itself.

Which dogs are most at risk

MMVD accounts for roughly 75% of all acquired heart disease in dogs. The risk profile is well defined:

  • Small and toy breeds: Yorkshire Terrier, Miniature Poodle, Chihuahua, Jack Russell, Dachshund
  • Cavalier King Charles Spaniel: the breed with the strongest known genetic predisposition, often with a murmur before the age of five
  • Cocker Spaniels and other small spaniel types
  • Older dogs generally: prevalence reaches around 85% in dogs over 13

If your dog is small and past eight, a stethoscope check should be part of every routine appointment. It is not fussing — it is what changes the outcome.

The Signs Worth Acting On

The frustrating thing about this disease is how quiet it is. By the time a symptom shows up, the heart has been working overtime for a long while.

Watch for:

  • A dry cough, especially at night, in the early morning, or just after getting up
  • Tiring on walks — sitting down, stopping, wanting to turn back when they never used to
  • Fast or laboured breathing at rest, with no heat and no exercise to explain it
  • A swollen abdomen, typical when the right side of the heart is affected
  • Fainting or sudden collapse, often linked to excitement or exertion
  • Pale or bluish gums
  • Unexplained weight loss despite eating normally

Rapid breathing at rest, bluish gums or a collapse are not wait-until-Monday problems. They are emergencies.

The Stages: Why Treatment Often Starts Before Symptoms

Vets classify this disease in stages, and knowing your dog's stage tells you why they are on the medication they are on.

B1Murmur present, heart not enlargedNoUsually not
B2Murmur plus an enlarged heart on imagingNoYes — this is where it starts
CHas, or has had, congestive heart failureYesYes, full combination
DNo longer responding to standard treatmentYes, severeYes, higher or combined doses

The turning point is stage B2. That is where a vet may start medication before any symptom appears — and where treatment has the greatest effect.

Why start treating a dog that looks perfectly well?

Because there is solid evidence for it.

The EPIC study, the largest randomised clinical trial ever run in veterinary cardiology, followed 360 dogs with MMVD and an enlarged heart who had no symptoms yet.

Dogs given pimobendan took a median of 462 days longer — around 15 months — to develop congestive heart failure than dogs given a placebo. Overall survival was significantly longer too.

Fifteen months. For an eleven-year-old dog, that is not a statistic. That is another summer.

The Medications — and Why the Active Ingredient Matters More Than the Brand

This is the part that confuses most newly diagnosed owners, and it is also where the money is.

Knowing the active ingredient, not just the brand name, is the single most useful thing you can learn as the owner of a dog with heart disease. The active ingredient is what treats your dog. The same active ingredient often appears under several brand names at very different prices.

Pimobendan

The cornerstone of treatment. It does two things at once: increases the force of the heart's contraction and relaxes the blood vessels, reducing the work the heart has to do.

You will most likely see it as Vetmedin (the originator brand), but pimobendan is also available in the UK as VMD-authorised generic products from other manufacturers. Same active ingredient, different label, often a different price.

Typical dosing is twice daily, given about an hour before food.

ACE inhibitors — benazepril, enalapril, ramipril

These lower the pressure the heart works against and slow the hormonal cycle that wears the heart out faster. Given once or twice daily, long term, with periodic blood tests to monitor kidney function.

Diuretics — furosemide, torasemide

Used when fluid is accumulating in the lungs. Your dog will drink and urinate noticeably more. That is expected, and it is never a reason to restrict their water.

Spironolactone

A potassium-sparing diuretic with an additional protective effect on the heart muscle, usually added at stages C and D.

Because these are daily, repeat, lifelong prescriptions, a small difference in price per pack becomes a large difference over your dog's remaining years.

The Number Nobody Shows You

A small dog at stage C is commonly on two to four medicines at once, every day, indefinitely. Add periodic monitoring — imaging, blood tests, blood pressure — and the ongoing commitment becomes clear.

And here is where many owners make the single most dangerous decision available to them: they stretch the doses to make a pack last longer. Or skip a few days. Or buy whatever appeared first without checking.

Cardiac medication works by maintaining a steady concentration in the bloodstream. Missing doses does not save money — it undoes the very thing you are paying for, and an emergency admission costs far more than the pack ever did.

The answer is not to take less medicine. It is to pay less for the same medicine. There are clear structural reasons why the same pet medicine costs different amounts across UK pharmacies, and understanding them is what turns a monthly shock into a managed budget.

3 Ways to Pay Less — Without Compromising Care

1. Ask your vet for a written prescription

In the UK you are legally entitled to ask your vet for a written prescription and buy the medication from a VMD-registered pharmacy of your choice. A vet cannot refuse to write one for a medicine they are already prescribing to your animal.

Buying from your own practice's dispensary is convenient. It is frequently not the cheapest option. For a lifelong daily treatment, this one request is the largest single saving available to most owners.

2. Ask about the same active ingredient under a different brand

This is the lever almost nobody uses. Because pimobendan is available from more than one manufacturer, there may be an equally suitable, VMD-authorised product with the same active ingredient at a lower price.

Only your vet can decide what is appropriate, and a UK prescription is written for a specific product. But you are entitled to the conversation. "Is there an equivalent with the same active ingredient that might cost less?" is a fair, sensible question, and a good vet will welcome it.

3. Sort out insurance before a diagnosis, not after

Here is the timing most owners get wrong. Once heart disease is on the record it becomes a pre-existing condition, and cover for it becomes very hard to arrange.

If you have a young dog, or an at-risk breed like a Cavalier King Charles Spaniel, this matters now rather than later. It is worth weighing the honest pros and cons of pet insurance for older pets while you still have the choice — because the whole point of insurance is to cover exactly the expensive, ongoing conditions you cannot predict.

The Free Test That Tells You Before Your Dog Does

This is the most valuable thing you can do at home, and it costs nothing.

With your dog asleep or fully relaxed, count how many times their chest rises in 30 seconds and double it. A stable dog sits below 30 breaths per minute.

Write it down every day. When that number starts climbing consistently — say from 22 to 34 over several days — it is an early sign of fluid building in the lungs, and it lets you phone your vet before a breathing crisis. Many diuretic adjustments are made on exactly this reading.

The rest of the home routine

  • Cut the salt hard: no table scraps, no processed meat, no cheese, no crisps
  • Short, frequent walks rather than one long one, avoiding the heat of the day
  • Keep them lean — excess weight loads the heart directly
  • Never restrict water, even on diuretics
  • Set phone alarms for medication times; consistency is half the treatment
  • Keep the house calm — prolonged barking and excitement raise cardiac demand

Frequently Asked Questions

How long can a dog live with congestive heart failure?

It varies widely with stage, breed and response to treatment. Many dogs live comfortably for one to three years after a congestive heart failure diagnosis with consistent medication and monitoring. Dogs treated earlier, at stage B2, typically do better than those diagnosed once symptoms appear.

What is the first sign of heart failure in a dog?

Usually a heart murmur detected by your vet during a routine examination, often years before your dog seems unwell. The first sign owners themselves notice tends to be a dry cough at night or unusual tiredness on familiar walks.

Can heart failure in dogs be cured?

No. Mitral valve disease is managed rather than cured. Medication slows progression, controls fluid build-up and extends both lifespan and quality of life, but the underlying valve damage is permanent — which is why treatment continues for life.

How much does heart medication for dogs cost in the UK?

It depends on your dog's weight, the number of medicines prescribed and where you buy. What is consistent is the spread: identical products can differ substantially in price between pharmacies, and originator brands typically cost more than VMD-authorised generics with the same active ingredient.

Can I buy Vetmedin without a prescription in the UK?

No. Pimobendan is a POM-V medicine and legally requires a veterinary prescription. You can, however, ask your vet for a written prescription and buy from any VMD-registered pharmacy rather than the practice dispensary.

Should I stop my dog's heart medication if he seems better?

No — and this is important. Feeling better is the medication working. Stopping or spacing doses allows fluid and cardiac strain to return, often rapidly and sometimes as an emergency. Only your vet should change the dose.

The Bottom Line

A heart failure diagnosis is frightening. But this is one of the chronic conditions that responds best to treatment in all of veterinary medicine. Diagnosed at the right stage, medicated consistently and monitored properly, many dogs go on for years — walking, playing, and taking your side of the sofa exactly as they always did.

What makes the difference is not luck. It is consistency: the dose on time, the breaths counted, the check-up booked. And yes, the treatment fitting your budget every month — because the treatment you can sustain is the only one that works.
